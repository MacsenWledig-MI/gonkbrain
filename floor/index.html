The Floor

A society rich enough to prevent destitution has no right to use destitution as leverage.

The idea in one paragraph

Every society wealthy enough to do it should guarantee a floor no one falls below: a home, enough food, medical care, and an education. Not as charity, but as the precondition for everything else. You cannot fully be a worker, a citizen, or a free person without it.

Housing

A home of your own, not a shelter bed.

Food

Enough to eat, unconditionally.

Medical care

Full coverage. No bill at the point of care.

Education

College, a trade or an apprenticeship.

Universal means universal

Everyone who lives here gets it: no means tests, no sorting the deserving from the undeserving. Universal programs like Social Security endure; programs that sort people get stigmatized and cut. Everyone pays in, too, by income, on the tax return, never at an office window.

The Floor does not abolish incentive. It abolishes desperation as an employment policy.

Why it doesn't exist yet

Cost and politics are part of it. Another part is that desperation does real work in our economy. When survival depends on taking any job on any terms, employers don't have to make jobs worth choosing. That gap is a hidden subsidy, paid by the people with the least and collected by everyone who buys their labor cheap. The Floor ends it. Expect opposition from those who benefit, dressed as arithmetic. Below is the arithmetic.

The full bill

Total: $2.2 trillion a year in new public spending at full operation (year eight; health in four), 7% of GDP; $1.8 trillion of it replaces premiums, bills and state Medicaid payments that end. Net new cost to the country: close to zero. Funding: contributions that replace premiums (7.5% of payroll from employers, 4% of income above $40,000 from households, the states' Medicaid share continued), plus taxes on capital, wealth and tax breaks. Nearly every household comes out ahead; the top tenth and the health industry's overhead pay for it.

Two answers to keep in your pocket

“How will you pay for it?”

It's costed. The pamphlet's second and third pages carry the bill and the revenue; the economic package carries every figure and the page it came from. The country already spends more on social provision, public and private combined, than France. The Floor changes the channel, not the amount.

“That's communism.”

No caseworker, no judgment, no office window: the Floor is the least intrusive form of provision ever proposed. It is the means-tested state that interrogates you. Universal benefit and universal contribution are one principle, and it is the same one Social Security has run on since 1935.
