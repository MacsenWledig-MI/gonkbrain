Where the cash runs out. In this model a family of four with an employer family plan comes out ahead to about $170,000 of income; a single person on an employer single plan to about $45,000 to $70,000, depending on how much of the employer’s saving reaches their pay; a single wage earner with no coverage and no rent burden to about $30,000, because the 7.5% on their pay now buys coverage this ledger gives no dollar value to; renters and families keep gaining further up through the rent guarantee and the food credit. Above those lines the Floor costs more than it returns in cash. The lines sit lower for one-person households than for families because the contributions scale with income while the guarantees scale with people. Not everyone comes out ahead, and the design does not pretend otherwise.

What this leaves out

The cap on itemized deductions, the taxation of capital gains at death and the wealth tax are not modeled. They fall almost entirely on households above about $250,000 that itemize or hold appreciated assets, or above $50 million of net worth, and would make the result for those households worse than shown.

The VAT line is an estimate: 5% on the broad base (about 60% of consumption), with consumption assumed to be 95% of income under $50,000, falling to 50% above $400,000. The Floor's own card and rent guarantee offset most of the VAT for the poorest households; a one-time rise in the price level of about 3% is not shown.

Existing taxes and the EITC phase-out still apply on top of the Floor's single taper, so effective marginal rates for families just above poverty remain in the 50s, down from the 70s without the taper. The rent guarantee is modeled on HUD's national fair market rent for a modest home; your area's figure is higher or lower. Owners are not modeled; the Floor's owner backstop is a property-tax circuit breaker for severely burdened owners below median income.

Prices: the package assumes supply programs hold rents and food prices roughly steady and that the health system pays providers at CBO's lower rates. If rates end up near today's, the federal bill is about $630 billion higher and the package as designed does not cover it. Nothing here is a forecast of your taxes; it is one design, applied to one household, with its sources open.
