The bill in one page

The Floor costs the federal budget about $2.3 trillion a year in new spending once its four guarantees are running, 7.4% of GDP, if the health system pays hospitals and doctors at the lower of the two payment rates the Congressional Budget Office has modeled. At payment rates near today's average it costs about $2.9 trillion, 9.4% of GDP. When long-term care enters in 2032, add $600 billion to either figure. Most of the money is not new to the country: about $1.9 trillion of the core bill is the health care bill that households, employers and states already pay through premiums, medical bills and state Medicaid budgets, moved onto one ledger. At the lower payment rates the country spends about $645 billion a year less on health care in total, mostly insurer and billing overhead and lower prices; at today's rates the saving is about $40 billion. The spending that is new in real terms is homes built, groceries bought, seats filled and a smoother taper for families just above poverty: about $500 billion a year.

It is paid for in three tiers. Contributions that replace the premiums they abolish raise $1.36 trillion: 7.5% of payroll from employers above their first $2 million of wages, 5% of income above $20,000 (single) or $40,000 (joint) from households, and the states' Medicaid share, continued. A progressive package aimed at capital, wealth and tax expenditures raises $560 billion. A closing tier raises $490 billion, and its largest piece is a 5% value-added tax, the instrument every European floor uses; a 7% household contribution in place of the 5% is the alternative. Against the $2.29 trillion bill at lower payment rates, the three tiers raise $2.40 trillion, a margin of about $115 billion that exists to absorb the optimism built into static revenue scoring. If the wealth tax is struck down, the package is about $65 billion short. At today's payment rates it does not balance at all: the shortfall is about $510 billion a year, and this document states it rather than hiding it, because the two state plans that reached this point, Vermont's in 2014 and Colorado's in 2016, died when their financing was published.

Most households come out ahead in cash; not all do. A family of four earning $80,000 with an employer family plan trades about $10,500 a year in premiums and medical bills for a $2,000 contribution and about $1,900 in VAT, and gains a share of the $14,000 its employer stops spending on its behalf: $7,000 to $18,000 a year better off. A single worker on an employer single plan is ahead to about $45,000 to $70,000 of income and behind above that; a single earner at $150,000 pays about $9,000 to $11,000 more than the Floor returns. A family of four at $250,000 is about $5,000 to $6,000 behind; at $1 million, $85,000 to $115,000 behind. The contributions scale with income and the guarantees scale with people, so the line where the Floor stops paying for itself sits lower for one-person households than for families, and the design does not pretend otherwise.

The evidence on work says hours fall a few percent and nobody stops. On growth the honest reading of CBO's modeling is that a floor financed mostly by taxes on labor and consumption leaves GDP a few percent below its path, not above it, with the health system's own savings and better health pulling the other way. On the OECD's measure the United States already spends more on social provision, public and private combined, than France; the Floor changes the channel more than the amount. The transition takes eight years, revenue first, the cheap guarantees in year one, health over four years, housing last because the homes have to be built, long-term care in year six.

Design rules that drive the numbers

Seven choices decide what the Floor costs. Each is a design decision, not a finding, and each can be argued with; the costing below follows from them.

Universal in right, income-rated in contribution. Everyone who lives here is covered by residency, with no application to prove need. Where a guarantee has a per-person price (a home, groceries), the contribution scales with income through the tax return, the way Social Security contributions do. A household in the top half is fully covered and pays its own way; a household whose income falls is covered the same month, with no office visit. The sorting the pamphlet refuses happens in no office at all; it happens in the tax code, where it already happens to everyone.

Goods and services, not cash. Care is delivered by providers paid by one public payer; homes are delivered as leases; food as a grocery credit on a card; education as a seat. In-kind delivery keeps the inflation channel narrow, lets supply be planned, and is the point of the Floor as written: the survival question is removed, not converted into a budgeting problem.

Supply is part of the policy. Every guarantee that could raise prices is paired with a capacity program: a public building program for housing, provider capacity and price negotiation for health care, logistics and waste recovery for food, seats and instructors for college. Price pressure is answered with units, not with caps.

Federal guarantee, local delivery. The entitlement is federal and the money is federal. Delivery runs through institutions that already exist (hospitals, housing authorities, school districts, community colleges), with a direct federal channel wherever a state declines to deliver, as a dozen states declined Medicaid expansion.

Each pillar carries its own revenue. At maturity the package is pay-as-you-go: every guarantee is paired with named taxes that cover it, so no pillar is an unfunded promise. Capital spending (building homes) is borrowed and repaid from rents and the budget, the way roads and water systems are financed.

Residency is a parameter, not a loophole. The costing covers the whole Census resident population, including the roughly 11 to 14 million people without legal status, who are 3 to 4 percent of the base and of the bill. Excluding them would save about 3 percent and would require status checks at every clinic, school and grocery register. How long someone must live here before coverage begins is a political choice, costed at the margin rather than buried.

One accounting convention. Costs are annual, in 2025 dollars. CBO's single-payer estimates are for 2030, but CBO's projected 2030 economy (about $30.8 trillion) is the size of the actual 2025 economy ($30.86 trillion), so its 2030 dollar figures are used at 2025 scale without adjustment. Revenue is at 2025 scale: CBO's own figure for the first full-effect year of each option (2026, or 2027 for the options that start in January 2026), deflated to 2025 by CBO's projected nominal growth of about 4% a year; the employer levy is computed on 2025 wages. Two of CBO's December 2024 options were scored on a baseline in which the 2017 tax act expired after 2025, which the July 2025 law has since made permanent; those lines are counted lower and say so. Each pillar is shown three ways: gross, offsets, and net new public spending. Where the evidence disagrees, the range is shown rather than a point.

Medical care: the largest number, and a saving at one price and not the other

Universal care moves about $1.8 trillion a year of health spending from households, employers and states onto the federal ledger at CBO's lower payment rates, or $2.4 trillion at rates near today's average. At the lower rates the country spends about $645 billion a year less on health care in total; at the higher rates about $40 billion less. It is the pillar that costs the budget the most and the country the least, and the one whose cost depends most on a single political decision: what hospitals get paid.

What it is. One public payer covers every resident for hospital, physician, drugs, mental health, maternity, dental, vision and hearing care, with no premiums, deductibles or copays, including on prescriptions. Hospitals move to global budgets and drug prices are negotiated. Long-term services and supports come in from 2032 under their own contribution. In CBO's terms, Floor Health is its Option 3 with one addition: Option 3 already covers dental, vision and hearing and charges nothing out of pocket for covered medical services, but keeps a 3% cost share on retail drugs, which the Floor removes, about $15 billion a year. Option 4 is the same system at higher payment rates, and Option 5 is Option 4 with long-term care.

The two payment rates. Under CBO's lower-rate options, hospitals are paid 123% of Medicare's rates, which is 87% of the all-payer average they receive today and half of what private insurers pay; physicians are paid 111% of Medicare, 93% of today's average. Under the higher-rate options, hospitals get 142% of Medicare and physicians 120%, roughly today's averages. The lower rates are a 13% cut in average hospital payment and a 7% cut for physicians, and they are where the $645 billion saving comes from. The package is costed at the lower rates because that is the system whose financing closes; the higher-rate case is carried through every table because it is the one the hospital lobby will fight for.

The arithmetic. CBO projected 2030 health spending of $6.63 trillion under current law, with federal subsidies of $2.82 trillion. Option 3 raises federal subsidies by $1,769 billion and cuts national health spending by $650 billion; Option 4 raises them by $2,397 billion and cuts spending by $42 billion; out-of-pocket spending falls by $306 to $327 billion of the $721 billion projected. Adding zero drug cost sharing gives the Floor's lines: federal spending up $1,785 billion at lower rates and $2,412 billion at higher, national spending down $645 billion or $37 billion. Long-term care (Option 5 less Option 4) later adds $599 billion to the federal figure and $332 billion to national spending, and ends another $139 billion of out-of-pocket nursing costs. CBO's federal figure counts the tax exclusion for employer coverage as a subsidy that ends when premiums become taxable wages, so it is already net of that revenue; the employer contribution in the funding section is counted net of the same effect, to avoid claiming the money twice.

Where the money already is. In 2024 the country spent $5.28 trillion on health, 18.0% of GDP and $15,474 a person. Employers paid $753 billion in premiums; households paid about $600 billion in premiums and $557 billion out of pocket; state and local governments paid $335 billion toward Medicaid. Under Floor Health the employer premium and the household premium go to zero, about $300 billion of the out-of-pocket spending ends (the rest is over-the-counter items, nursing-home private pay until 2032, and other care the system does not cover), and the states' Medicaid share continues as a maintenance-of-effort payment to the national plan, $260 billion before long-term care transfers and $335 billion after. Those streams, about $1.9 trillion a year in the core phase and $2.1 trillion at full operation, are what the contributions in the funding section replace. The federal government already runs the largest share: Medicare's net outlays were $988 billion in fiscal 2025 and federal Medicaid $627 billion, plus the tax exclusion for employer coverage, which CBO values at $471 billion in 2026 counting income and payroll taxes.

Why the total falls. Administration is 34% of U.S. health spending, $812 billion in 2017 against 17% in Canada; U.S. insurers' overhead runs $844 a person against $146. Traditional Medicare administers care at about 2% of spending and private insurers at 12%, the ratio CBO builds on: payers' administrative spending falls about $410 billion in every CBO option. Prices are the other half, and they are the half that depends on the payment-rate decision. The OECD's 2024 figures put U.S. spending at $14,885 a person against $9,365 in Germany and $7,367 in France, for a life expectancy of 79.0 against the OECD's 81.1. Independent models find larger savings than CBO: the PERI team's 9.6%, the Lancet model's 13% and 68,000 fewer deaths a year. CBO is the conservative estimate and the one this package uses.

The baseline is getting worse, which raises the gross bill and not the net. The July 2025 reconciliation law cuts federal Medicaid by $911 billion over ten years and leaves 10 million more people uninsured by 2034; the enhanced marketplace credits expired at the end of 2025 and add 4.2 million more. CBO now expects 30 million uninsured in 2026, 37 million by 2036. Every one of them is new coverage for Floor Health to pay for, so the federal line is larger against today's baseline than against 2020's; the country's total spending is what it is either way.

The precedents. Vermont enacted a single-payer framework in 2011 and abandoned it in December 2014, when the financing came back as an 11.5% payroll tax and an income-based premium rising to 9.5%. Colorado's Amendment 69, a 10% payroll tax for a state plan, lost 79 to 21 in 2016. The Floor's health financing (7.5% from employers, 5% from households, plus the long-term care contribution later) is in the same range. The difference the package can claim is arithmetic, not magic: the contributions replace premiums that cost the median family more, and the document says exactly who pays more.

Transition. Four years. Year one: drug price negotiation across the market, and the uninsured enrolled automatically in Medicare-administered coverage. Year two: Medicare for everyone under 26 and over 55, with the contributions phased in by cohort. Year three: everyone. Year four: cost sharing to zero. Two costs belong to the transition and not to the steady state. About 1.8 million people work in insurance and provider billing (PERI's estimate: roughly 800,000 in insurers, a million in provider administration); a just-transition fund on PERI's terms, a year of wage replacement plus $10,000 for retraining and $10,000 for relocation, is about $60 billion spread over the phase-in, and the Floor itself is what those households fall back on. Hospitals that live on private rates need a corridor: a five-year stabilization fund of about $20 billion a year for rural and safety-net hospitals, which is cheaper than the closures it prevents.

Housing: the largest new cost

The housing guarantee costs about $200 billion a year in net new public spending at full operation, range $150 to 250 billion. The tax code spends about $145 billion a year on homeowners through the mortgage interest and property tax deductions and the exclusion of home-sale gains, and about $300 billion if the untaxed rental value of owner-occupied homes is counted as Treasury does.

The guarantee is one promise delivered by three instruments. Nobody sleeps outside or in a shelter who wants a home (Housing First). Nobody pays more than 30% of income for a modest home (the rent guarantee). And the homes exist to be guaranteed (a public building program). The order matters: the third instrument is what keeps the second from being a rent subsidy that landlords capture.

Housing First. On one night in January 2025, 745,652 people were homeless, down from 771,480 the year before and the first decline in the count since 2016. More than 1.3 million people used a shelter at some point in 2022, so the yearly flow is roughly double the nightly count. Permanent supportive housing costs about $20,100 a household a year and rapid rehousing about $8,500; the National Alliance to End Homelessness put the cost of offering Housing First to every shelter household at $9.6 billion a year, and the figure here adds the unsheltered and outreach. Houston cut homelessness 62% in a decade this way. The offsets are real but should not be sold as free money: the Community Guide's review found $1.80 of public benefit per dollar in U.S. studies, while RAND's 2025 review notes that the most rigorous designs find smaller offsets. The money saved lands in county hospitals and jails, not in the program's budget, so the bill below counts the full cost. Finland is the other warning: after eleven years of decline its homelessness rose 20% in 2025 once funding was cut. A guarantee has to stay funded to stay a guarantee.

The rent guarantee. Today 22.7 million renter households (49%) pay more than 30% of income for housing and 12.1 million pay more than half; 13.8 million income-eligible households get no assistance, because vouchers are a lottery that reaches about one eligible household in four. The Floor makes the voucher an entitlement: any resident household whose rent for a modest home exceeds 30% of income receives the difference, set from tax data, portable, with landlords required to take it. The payment standard is HUD's fair market rent, the 40th percentile of local rents ($1,465 a month for one bedroom nationally in 2025), not whatever the landlord asks. The guarantee is full for households up to 60% of area median income and tapers to zero at 80%, which is the band today's vouchers serve plus one tier above it. Fully funding vouchers for every household now eligible would add about $118 billion a year (Urban Institute, 2023) and lift 6.4 million people out of poverty; the line below adds the 60-to-80 tier. The evidence that vouchers raise rents where vacancy is low (Collinson and Ganong; Susin; Eriksen and Ross) is why the standard is the 40th percentile and why the entitlement starts in year four, after the building program has delivered. The 3.87 million homes vacant and for rent in the third quarter of 2026, a 7.6% rental vacancy rate, up sharply from 2022, mean many markets can absorb the demand now; the hot ones are exactly where the building program goes first.

The building program. Every serious estimate of the shortage lands between 3.7 and 4.7 million homes (Freddie Mac 3.7M, Zillow 4.7M, Up for Growth 3.8M, Realtor.com 4.0M). A federal Housing Development Authority, on the design of the 2024 Homes Act ($30 billion a year, a revolving fund, up to 1.3 million homes, rents capped by income), is sized here to 2 million permanently affordable, mixed-income homes over ten years: 200,000 a year, about 13% on top of the 1.5 million homes completed in 2025. The Faircloth amendment, which caps the number of public housing units at their 1999 level, is repealed in the same bill. At $250,000 to 350,000 a home all-in (Texas builds market-rate apartments near $150,000 a unit, California near $430,000), that is $50 to 70 billion a year of construction, financed by the Authority's bonds, about $600 billion outstanding after a decade, serviced from rents with a budget appropriation that rises to about $15 billion a year by year ten. The budget also carries a $30 billion yearly capital grant for the deeply affordable share and $10 billion for repairing the public housing that exists. Vienna, the model, houses more than 60% of its residents in municipal or limited-profit homes. As these homes come online with rents set by income, the rent guarantee's subsidy bill falls; the two lines trade against each other over the decade.

What the tax code already does for housing. The mortgage interest deduction cost $52.6 billion in tax year 2025 (up from $25.6 billion after the 2025 law raised the SALT cap); the property-tax deduction about $29 billion a year and the exclusion of home-sale gains about $63 billion a year on JCT's 2025–29 estimates: about $145 billion. Treasury also counts the untaxed imputed rent of owner-occupied homes, $151.6 billion in fiscal 2025, which takes the total to about $300 billion. Vouchers got $38.4 billion.

Capacity. Construction employed 8.36 million people in September 2026, and the industry says it needs 349,000 more in 2026 before any new program. At the industry's rule of thumb (about 3,450 jobs per $1 billion), 200,000 homes a year needs 170,000 to 240,000 workers, which is why the program ramps over four years, why the education pillar's apprenticeship fund points at the trades, and why tariffs that add about $10,900 to a home are a housing policy too.

Food: a card every resident holds

The food guarantee costs about $140 billion a year in net new public spending, on top of the $103 billion SNAP spent in fiscal 2025. The range is $110 to 180 billion, and the whole spread comes from one dial: where the credit tapers to zero.

What it is. Every resident holds a Floor card, usable for groceries and, for people without a kitchen, prepared meals. The monthly credit is USDA's Thrifty Food Plan for the household's size: $1,024 a month for a family of four in September 2026, about $256 a person. It is full for households at or below the poverty line and tapers to zero at 250% of poverty. The card is issued by residency, not by application. The full credit is the default for anyone with no tax return on file; the taper applies only when a return shows income, with an immediate reset when someone reports an income drop and reconciliation at tax time. No work test, no asset test, no time limit. Sending every resident the full credit and recovering it through the income tax would cost the Treasury the same net amount; the taper is the same arithmetic with smaller gross flows.

Why it costs more per person than SNAP. SNAP paid an average of $189 a person a month in fiscal 2025 because benefits are cut by 30 cents for every dollar of counted income. The Floor pays the full plan to the poor and tapers above them. The starting line is also lower than it was: the July 2025 reconciliation law extended work requirements to age 64, shifted benefit costs to states from 2028 and cut $187 billion over ten years, and participation had already fallen by about 4.7 million people by March 2026.

School meals. Federal lunch and breakfast programs cost $25.0 billion in fiscal 2025 and already serve 73% of lunches free or reduced-price; nine states have made meals free for every child. Covering the remaining paid meals nationally adds roughly $13 billion a year, close to the $11 billion the 2024 federal bill was priced at and well under the $30 billion sometimes quoted.

The waste side of the pillar. The U.S. generated 70 million tons of surplus food in 2024, 29% of the supply, worth $380 billion, while fewer than 13% of donatable surplus was donated. ReFED's full menu of fixes needs about $14 billion a year of investment for a decade and returns roughly $73 billion a year, mostly to businesses and households rather than the Treasury. So recovery does not fund the credit; it is this pillar's supply program, and the public share (grants for donation logistics and organics capacity, a date-label standard that costs almost nothing) is the $4 billion line above.

The gap being closed. In 2024, 47.9 million people lived in food-insecure households, 13.7% of households; 14.1 million of them were children. USDA ended that survey in 2025, so the Floor has to carry its own measurement: a yearly food-security survey is a $50 million line that keeps the guarantee honest.

Education: tuition-free public college and training

Tuition- and fee-free public college plus a training guarantee cost about $73 billion a year in net new public spending, range $60 to 100 billion. It is the cheapest pillar, and it is cheap for a reason the pamphlet already states: the Floor's other three guarantees pay for the room, the food and the doctor, so this one only has to pay for the seat.

What it is. Public two- and four-year institutions charge resident undergraduates no tuition or fees, first dollar, with no income cap; nonresident and graduate tuition is unchanged. The federal government pays each institution a per-student amount in place of the tuition it now collects from residents, and states keep their appropriations as a condition of the money: $130.7 billion in fiscal 2025, which the guarantee locks in rather than replaces. Pell continues ($38 billion, 7.3 million students) but as a living-cost grant, no longer a tuition grant. Training is the same guarantee at a shorter length: free credentials and short programs at community colleges (the Workforce Pell that began in July 2026 covers 150- to 599-hour programs; the Floor makes it universal and unconditional), plus an apprenticeship fund sized to triple the 702,000 active apprentices and roughly triple WIOA's $3.2 billion in adult, dislocated-worker and youth training.

The 60% share is an assumption, and it is the main uncertainty in this pillar: SHEEO's net tuition figure includes graduate and nonresident students, who pay more per head, and no public source splits it cleanly. Georgetown's Center on Education and the Workforce priced a first-dollar, no-income-cap design covering more students at $58.2 billion in its first year in 2016 dollars, about $77 billion today, which is the top of this pillar's range. Their stronger claim, that the added tax revenue from more graduates exceeds the program's annual cost within ten years, held for the income-capped design they modeled; it is the best pays-for-itself claim in the whole package, and it is a model result, not an observation.

What the Floor makes unnecessary. Proposals for debt-free college run to $75 billion (2016 dollars) because they must cover living costs. Here those costs are the housing and food pillars' job, so the education guarantee stays close to the price of tuition itself. The $1.7 trillion owed by 42.3 million borrowers is the stock that this flow stops growing; what to do with the stock is a separate decision and is not in this bill.

Not in the base. The pamphlet's pillar runs from public K–12 through college. Extending it down to early childhood is the obvious next guarantee: universal pre-K for 3- and 4-year-olds is about $74 billion a year at 90% participation (NIEER), and a child-care guarantee capping family costs at 7% of income was scored by CBO at roughly $27 billion a year averaged over its first decade, more at maturity. Together they would add $100 to 130 billion a year; they are listed as an extension, not counted in the totals.

The single taper: the price of not building a trap

A family of four between the poverty line and 250% of it would face, under the pillars as drawn, a food credit tapering at about 26 cents on the dollar and a rent guarantee that takes 30 cents of each new dollar, on top of the 5% health contribution, ordinary income and payroll taxes, and the phase-out of the EITC. Stacked, those can take 70 cents or more of a raise, in a system where families with children just above poverty already face a median marginal rate of 51%. The fix is a single Floor taper: one schedule, administered on the tax return, that caps the combined withdrawal of the food credit and the rent guarantee at 25 cents per dollar of income above the poverty line and tops the bundle back up where the separate tapers would take more.

It costs about $90 billion a year, range $60 to 120 billion. The figure is a model estimate from the household ledger published alongside this document, applied to the income distribution, rounded up; no outside source has costed this design. It is inside the core bill, not an extra. It brings combined effective marginal rates for families just above poverty from the 70s into the 50s. It does not bring them lower than that, because the income tax, the payroll tax and the EITC phase-out still apply, and the document should not claim otherwise.

The full bill

The Floor costs the federal budget about $2.3 trillion a year in its core form at CBO's lower payment rates, 7.4% of 2025 GDP, and about $2.9 trillion, 9.4%, at today's rates. At full operation in 2035, with long-term care, the figures are $2.9 and $3.5 trillion. The country's real resources are a different question: at the lower rates the core Floor costs the country about $140 billion less than it spends now, because the health system's saving exceeds the new spending on homes, food, seats and the taper; at today's rates it costs about $470 billion more, 1.5% of GDP. Both sentences are true, and the pamphlet needs both.

The housing, food and education pillars do replace some private spending (excess rent, grocery bills, tuition), but that spending is what households keep, not a saving to the economy, so it is counted as a gain to households in the ledger section rather than netted here. The last column is the difference once the health system's own savings are counted, and it is the answer to the question of whether paying for the Floor breaks the economy: at the lower payment rates the Floor is a redistribution of about seven percent of GDP through the budget with no net increase in what the country consumes; at today's rates it is that redistribution plus a real increase of about 1.5% of GDP, and at full operation with long-term care, 0.6% to 2.6%.

On the OECD's measure, the Floor takes U.S. public social spending from 19.8% of GDP to about 26% in its core form and 28% at full operation, Germany's 27.9% and France's 30.6% being the neighbors, and leaves total social spending, public and private combined, about where it already is: 33.2% of GDP, the highest in the OECD. The country is already paying for a floor. It is buying insurance company overhead and emergency rooms with the money.

Paying for it

The core bill is raised in three tiers: $1.36 trillion of contributions that replace the premiums and bills they abolish, $560 billion from a progressive package aimed at capital, wealth and tax expenditures, and $490 billion from a closing tier whose largest piece is a value-added tax. Yields are CBO's own first-full-year figures at 2025 scale where CBO has scored the measure, scaled from other estimates where it has not, and every line names its source and its year.

The three tiers raise $2.40 trillion against a core bill of $2.29 trillion at the lower payment rates: a margin of about $115 billion, 5% of the revenue. That margin is not spare money. CBO's yields are static; scaling a one-point option to five or seven points overstates the yield because avoidance grows with the rate; the hours effects in the next section would lower income and payroll receipts; the employer line's offset is a convention, not a measurement; and two lines are this package's own judgment where the 2025 tax law moved the ground under CBO's score. If the wealth tax is struck down or avoided, the package loses $180 billion and is about $65 billion short, which a 5.5% household contribution or a 6% VAT would cover. At today's payment rates the bill is $2.9 trillion and the three tiers fall about $510 billion short; closing it would take, for example, a household contribution of 7% and a VAT of 9%, which is in the territory of the financing Vermont published before it gave up. The document states this because the alternative is to be found out.

Long-term care, from 2032. The $599 billion federal increment is funded by its own contribution, about 4% of earnings paid like the Medicare tax (about $560 billion on 2025 earnings), plus the $75 billion long-term care share of state Medicaid that transfers to the national plan: about $635 billion against $599 billion. Germany's long-term care insurance runs on a 3.6% contribution and Japan's on a mix of premiums and taxes; neither covers everything the Floor's would.

What universal contribution means. The package does not close on the top 1% alone, and this document does not claim it does. Tier B falls on capital, wealth and tax expenditures and is paid overwhelmingly by the top tenth. Tier A and the VAT fall on everyone with income or spending, in proportion. The employer contribution, as CBO models such levies, comes out of wages over time, so it is a tax on workers whatever the statute says. Everyone pays in; everyone is covered; and the next section says who ends up ahead and who does not.

Deficits. The package is pay-as-you-go at maturity: the federal deficit was $1.78 trillion in fiscal 2025 with net interest above $1 trillion and debt at 100% of GDP, and the Floor is not designed to add to it. The one borrowing is the housing program's construction, $50 to 70 billion a year of bonds serviced from rents and a rising appropriation, which is investment in an asset the public keeps.

Who comes out ahead, and who does not

The figures below come from the household ledger published alongside this document, which anyone can run with their own numbers and whose constants are all sourced. Each result is a range: the low end assumes none of the employer's change in costs reaches the worker's pay, the high end assumes all of it does, after tax, which is how CBO models the end of employer premiums. The ledger counts cash only. It gives no dollar value to the coverage itself, to a home that is secure, or to a credit that does not have to be applied for, which is to say it undercounts the Floor for the people with the least.

A family of four earning $80,000 with an employer family plan now pays $6,850 toward a $26,993 family premium plus about $3,600 a year in medical bills for care the Floor would cover ($1,630 a person of total out-of-pocket spending, of which about $900 is for covered care); their employer pays another $20,143 they never see. Under the Floor the family's contribution is 5% of the $40,000 above the threshold, $2,000; the VAT on its spending is about $1,900; its premiums and bills are zero. The employer's 7.5% on $80,000 of pay is $6,000, so about $14,100 of compensation is freed, worth about $10,800 after tax if it reaches the paycheck. The family is between $7,000 and $18,000 a year better off, before any rent or tuition it no longer pays.

A single parent earning $30,000 with two children, on Medicaid, paying $1,400 in rent, gains about $15,000 to $17,000 a year, nearly all of it from the rent guarantee and the food credit; the contribution is $500. A single renter at $45,000 on an employer single plan, paying $1,200, is ahead $5,000 to $9,000. A retired couple at $40,000 on Medicare is ahead about $7,600: two Part B premiums and most medical bills end and a partial food credit begins, against about $1,100 of VAT and no contribution, since the joint threshold is $40,000. A self-employed person at $60,000 buying a $7,000 plan is ahead about $4,500.

A single worker on an employer single plan stops coming out ahead somewhere between $45,000 and $70,000 of income, depending on how much of the employer's saving reaches pay, because the premium that ends is small ($1,440 a year) and the contribution grows with every dollar. At $100,000 that worker is about $4,000 behind; at $150,000, $9,000 to $11,000 behind. A single wage earner with no coverage and no rent burden breaks even near $30,000: the 7.5% on their pay now buys coverage, which the ledger does not value, and nothing else ends. A family of four at $250,000 with an employer family plan pays a $10,500 contribution, about $4,900 in VAT, a $1,000 surtax and a little more in brackets, against $10,450 of premiums and bills that end and an employer pass-through of about $1,000: $5,000 to $6,000 a year behind, before the deductions cap, which would make it worse. At $1 million the contribution is $48,000, the surtax $16,000, the bracket change $15,000 and the VAT $15,000; the employer's levy on that salary exceeds the premium it saves by $55,000, most of which comes out of pay; the household is $85,000 to $115,000 behind, plus capital gains taxed at death. Above $50 million of wealth the wealth tax is the bill.

The pattern is the design, and it should be said plainly. The Floor is paid for by three groups: the health industry's overhead and prices ($645 billion a year at the lower payment rates), the top tenth through Tier B, and single earners and households above the middle through contributions that scale with income while the guarantees scale with people. Families, renters, the uninsured, the old and the poor gain; a childless worker on a decent employer plan at $75,000 does not, in cash, and gets in exchange coverage that cannot be lost with the job, a rent guarantee if income falls, and a food credit the month it does. Whether that trade is worth it to that worker is the honest political question, and this package puts a number on it instead of a slogan.

Macroeconomics: work, wages, prices, growth

The evidence says the Floor would cut hours worked by several percent, leave total U.S. social spending roughly where it already is, and in CBO's framework leave GDP a few percent below its path at the end of the transition, with the health system's savings and better health as the offsets. Collapse is not in the evidence. Neither is the claim, made in the October edition of this document, that the Floor would leave GDP within a point of its path; that claim rested on a financing design that replaced premiums without raising marginal tax rates, and the design here does raise them.

The country already spends this money. On the public measure the U.S. is a low social spender: 19.8% of GDP against an OECD average of 21.2% and France's 30.6%. Count private spending net of taxes, as the OECD also does, and the U.S. is the highest social spender in the OECD at 33.2% of GDP, above France (32.0%) and ten points above the OECD average (22.6%). The Floor is mostly a change of channel: about 5% of GDP of private health premiums and out-of-pocket bills moves onto the public ledger, and the non-health pillars add about 1.6% of GDP of new claims on real resources, against a health saving of 2.1% at the lower payment rates. Economies run at that level everywhere in Western Europe. The risk is the transition, not the destination.

Marginal rates. This is where the October edition was wrong and the correction matters. Today's employer premium is a fixed per-head cost, $26,993 a family plan in 2025 whether the worker earns $30,000 or $300,000, and the worker's share is a fixed deduction. Neither carries a marginal rate: the next dollar earned is not taxed to pay for health care. The Floor replaces them with a 5% household contribution and a 7.5% employer-side levy that CBO's framework treats as a tax on wages, so the marginal tax rate on labor rises by about 12.5 points for workers at employers above the exemption, by about 4 more points when the long-term care contribution begins in 2032, and by about 3 effective points through the VAT on what the wages buy, with a further 2 points above $100,000 single and $200,000 joint. Average burdens fall for most households, as the ledger section shows; marginal rates rise for nearly all of them. In CBO's model the second effect, not the first, is what moves hours and output.

Work. Every guaranteed-income experiment finds people work somewhat less, and none finds them stopping. In the 1970s negative-income-tax trials husbands cut hours about 7%, wives and single mothers about 17%. OpenResearch's $1,000-a-month study found labor market participation about 4 points lower and one to two fewer hours a week, with the largest increase in time going to leisure. Alaska's dividend produced no employment effect and more part-time work; Finland's experiment found employment slightly higher and mental strain lower; Denver's found no between-group differences at 24 months. CBO's modeling of single payer alone, financed by trimming other government purchases so that marginal tax rates do not rise, puts hours per person 3.6% to 4.7% lower in 2030, from the income effect of free care, with the labor force 1% to 4% smaller. Financing by taxes lowers hours further, and the Floor adds tapered benefits that have their own income effect, so the working assumption here is hours 5% to 7% lower, concentrated among caregivers, students, the sick and people over 60. The labor-market effect that matters more is on the other side of the table: when nobody has to take any job on any terms, low-wage employers compete on pay and conditions, and the price of restaurant meals, retail and care rises once by a few percent. That is the hidden subsidy on the pamphlet's first page being repaid.

Growth. CBO's five single-payer systems, financed by cutting other government purchases so that neither debt nor marginal rates rise, leave 2030 GDP between 0.3% lower and 1.8% higher than its path: administrative savings of $533 to 562 billion (1.8% of GDP), employer health costs converted to wages, better health. Financed instead by taxes, the picture changes sign. CBO's cheapest system (lower rates, higher cost sharing, no long-term care) financed by a payroll tax leaves GDP 1.4% below path, by a flat income tax 1.2% below, by a progressive income tax 2.8% below; its most expensive system, with long-term care, 6.7% to 10.4% below. Floor Health is more expensive than CBO's cheapest system and is financed mostly by levies on labor and consumption, so the honest expectation for the core Floor is GDP roughly 2% to 4% below its path, and further below once long-term care enters. Three things pull the other way, none of them enough to be sure of the sign: the VAT, corporate, wealth and deduction measures distort labor less than the single taxes CBO modeled; the health and productivity gains CBO counts are in the +0.3 to +1.8 range; and the Floor's supply programs are investment. What the package cannot claim is that it is free for growth. It is a choice to buy security with some output, and the document should price it as one.

Wages. CBO's finding that after-tax wages rise about 11.5% under single payer comes from employer premiums becoming wages with no new levy. Under the Floor the employer contribution takes most of that money (about $760 billion gross against $753 billion of premiums), so wages are roughly flat from the employer side in aggregate: workers at small firms gain, workers at large firms roughly break even. Household gains come instead from premiums and bills ending, net of the contribution and the VAT, as the ledger section shows.

Prices. The starting point is not benign: consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year to September 2026 (core 2.6%, shelter 3.2%) and the Fed held at 3¾–4% after a September increase, so the phasing has to respect the cycle. In-kind delivery narrows the channel. The food credit adds perhaps $75 billion of grocery demand once substitution is counted, against a system carrying a 29% surplus. Single payer sets prices and is disinflationary by construction. The VAT raises the price level once, by about 3%. The rent guarantee is the real inflation risk, since a subsidy on a fixed stock goes to landlords; the answers are the 7.6% rental vacancy rate it starts from, the 40th-percentile payment standard, the building program that is funded in year one and delivers from year three, and the four-year ramp. The bottleneck is the trades: an industry already short 349,000 workers is why the education pillar's apprenticeship fund exists.

The window. Goldman Sachs puts 6% to 7% of U.S. workers in jobs AI can displace, with unemployment half a point above trend during the transition and productivity 15% higher at full adoption; Stanford's 2025 data already show a 13% relative employment decline for 22- to 25-year-olds in the most exposed occupations. A Floor whose benefits switch on the month income falls is an automatic stabilizer at national scale: in a downturn, spending on food and rent flows to the households that spend every dollar, and nobody loses coverage with a job. That is the macroeconomic version of the pamphlet's fourth page. The productivity dividend goes the same direction: the end of job lock, 68,000 fewer deaths a year on the Lancet model, graduates who pay more tax, children who stay in one school. None of it is counted in the tables above.

Transition: eight years, revenue first

The order is the policy. The revenue package is enacted before any guarantee switches on, the cheap and visible guarantees come in the first year, health takes four years, housing takes the longest because homes have to be built, and long-term care waits for the health system to be running.

Transition timeline: revenue package enacted 2027; Floor card and food credit, free school meals and free community college live in 2027; Housing Development Authority chartered and the Faircloth cap repealed 2027, Housing First surge 2027–2030, 200,000 public homes a year from 2029, rent guarantee an entitlement from 2030; health: drug prices set and the uninsured enrolled 2027, Medicare for under-26s and over-55s 2028, everyone covered 2029, zero cost sharing 2030; long-term care from 2032; full operation January 2035.

Year one is deliberately front-loaded with the three guarantees that are cheap, fast and felt: the Floor card with the food credit, free school meals, and free community college, together about $170 billion, funded by the first revenue measures to take effect (the deductions cap, the corporate rate, the top brackets, the IRS), which raise more than that, so the first two years reduce the deficit. The Housing Development Authority is chartered the same year so that its first homes land in year three, before the rent guarantee becomes an entitlement in year four; a subsidy that arrives before supply goes to landlords. Health runs on its own four-year track and brings its own contributions with it: the employer and household contributions begin the year premiums end, cohort by cohort, so no household pays twice. Each pillar carries its revenue line into law in the same bill, and no pillar's benefits start before its money does. That is the discipline that keeps the package from becoming the inflation it is accused of.

Provider rates. Hospitals paid at 123% of Medicare lose money on patients who were privately insured, and rural and safety-net hospitals close first. The package picks CBO's lower rate and a five-year stabilization fund. If the political settlement lands near today's rates instead, the federal health line rises to $2.4 trillion, the country's total saving shrinks to $37 billion, and the three tiers are about $510 billion short; the system is still universal, and the financing has to be rebuilt. Vermont and Colorado are what that looks like when it is not.

Revenue is scored conventionally. CBO's option estimates do not include macroeconomic feedback. Penn Wharton's dynamic analysis of the wealth tax cut its yield by about 15%; something similar could apply to the capital gains lines; the hours effects above would lower income and payroll receipts by tens of billions. The $115 billion margin is the whole allowance for all of it, and the package should not be enacted without the margin.

Marginal rates and growth. The contributions raise marginal tax rates on labor by about 12.5 points, more later. In CBO's framework that costs output, probably several percent of GDP at full operation. The design choices that soften it (the VAT, the corporate and wealth measures, the single taper) are already in the package; the ones that would soften it further (financing more of the health pillar from general revenue, or keeping a flat per-head element in the contribution) trade progressivity for growth and are the right argument to have in the open.

Rent capture. A subsidy that arrives before supply goes to landlords. The package starts from a 7.6% rental vacancy rate, charters the building authority in year one, makes the rent guarantee an entitlement only in year four, and ties the subsidy to the 40th-percentile rent rather than to whatever the landlord asks. The sensitivity is still real: the guarantee's cost scales with the rents it is measured against.

Construction capacity. Two hundred thousand extra homes a year into an industry short 349,000 workers means wage pressure in the trades and competition with private building. Materials cost 40% more than in 2020 and tariffs add about $10,900 a home. The ramp is four years, the apprenticeship fund points at the trades, and the construction is bond-financed so that a cost overrun lengthens a repayment schedule rather than cutting a guarantee.

Health costs grow faster than the economy. CMS projects health spending rising 5.4% a year against 4.1% for GDP through 2034, to 20.6% of GDP. A single payer's advantage is that it can set a budget; if it does not, the health contributions have to rise a tenth or two of a point every year to keep pace. Whether Congress holds a global budget against providers is the political test of this pillar.

States. The maintenance-of-effort payment and local delivery assume states participate, and after 2012 they cannot be made to. The package has a direct federal channel for the ones that refuse, as a dozen refused Medicaid expansion; it costs more to run and it should be in the law from the start rather than improvised. A state that keeps its Medicaid bill rather than paying the national plan is the likeliest form of refusal, and $260 billion rests on the negotiation.

Residency. The costing includes everyone the Census counts. If Congress excludes people without legal status, the bill falls about 3% and every clinic, school and grocery register becomes a checkpoint, which has costs of its own and is the sorting the pamphlet refuses. The choice is political; the arithmetic is small either way.

Durability. Finland's homelessness rose 20% in 2025 after funding was cut; a guarantee is only as universal as its appropriation. The structural answers are the ones the pamphlet already gives: universality, mandatory rather than annual spending, dedicated contributions, and benefits indexed to the cost of the thing guaranteed rather than to a frozen dollar amount.

Measurement. USDA ended the food-security survey in 2025 and the homelessness count depends on volunteers one night a year. A guarantee that cannot be measured cannot be defended; the package budgets about $50 million a year for its own statistics.

What no number here captures. The pamphlet's deepest claim is that removing the survival question changes what people do with their minds. The experiments measure hours and well-being, and well-being rises everywhere; the larger effect, if it exists, is outside the evidence either way. This package does not count it as a benefit, and it does not need to in order to balance.
